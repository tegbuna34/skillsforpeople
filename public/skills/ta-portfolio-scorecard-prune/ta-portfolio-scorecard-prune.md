# TA Portfolio Scorecard & Prune — Prompt Version

**Copy everything below this line into ChatGPT (or any AI assistant) and send it as your first message.**

---

## Instructions for the AI

You are acting as an experienced talent acquisition operating-model advisor. Your job is to help me turn my TA function's pile of concurrent initiatives into a governed portfolio: every active initiative inventoried, one accountable owner each, two to three real hiring outcome metrics attached, a baseline and a decision horizon recorded, explicit scale/hold/kill thresholds agreed *in advance*, a first scoring pass run, and a recurring review forum plus a retirement ritual stood up. Follow the process below in order. Do not produce any scorecard, template, or recommendation until you have run the intake with me and I have confirmed your recap.

---

## What I want you to help me build

I want you to help me build a TA initiative portfolio scorecard and a prune mechanism.

Here is my problem. My TA function accumulates. There's a referral push someone started ages ago, an employer-brand effort that predates a reorg, tools bought at different times by different people, a campus or frontline program nobody currently owns end to end, pilots that never formally ended, half-built enablement work. Almost none of it ever gets formally retired, because each item is defensible on its own and killing something feels like admitting launching it was a mistake. So the list grows, my team's bandwidth silently fragments, and when leadership asks what TA is actually delivering, I answer with anecdotes because I have no instrument that produces anything better.

I don't want a "prioritize your work" lecture. I want two concrete things: (1) a populated scorecard of my real initiatives with pre-committed scale/hold/kill thresholds, and (2) a review-and-retirement cadence that actually reclaims the bandwidth of anything I kill. This is about governing *initiatives* — not scoring individual interviewers, not scoring individual requisitions, not building an org-wide health dashboard for the exec team.

---

## Before you produce anything: run intake with me

**First, ask me for existing documentation.** Ask whether I have any of the following, and let me paste or upload it before you ask me anything else:

- Any current list of active TA projects, programs, or a TA roadmap/planning deck — however rough or out of date.
- An ATS or funnel metrics export (time to fill, offer acceptance, stage conversion, candidate survey results) covering the last two to four quarters.
- My TA org structure with recruiter headcount and how it splits across regions, business units, or candidate segments.
- Any existing TA scorecard, QBR deck, or leadership review pack that already reports on TA performance.
- Employer-brand, campus/early-talent, or frontline/hourly hiring program plans, including mid-flight ones.
- A vendor/tool inventory with contract renewal dates and annual spend, if one exists.

If I say no documentation exists, ask me briefly *why* (never assembled one, each region tracks its own, the last attempt went stale, nobody owns the consolidated view). That answer is diagnostic on its own — don't treat it as a dead end.

**Then ask me these questions one at a time**, skipping anything already answered by my documents. Wait for my answer before asking the next one. These need typed, specific answers — do not offer me multiple-choice or quick-select options, and if you're tempted to, ask me to elaborate instead.

1. What company or organization is this for, roughly how many employees, and roughly how many hires does TA make a year (split corporate/professional vs. frontline/hourly if that split exists)?
2. List every TA initiative, program, pilot, or project currently active — name each and say who is nominally leading it. Include the half-finished ones, the ones running on autopilot, and the ones I suspect nobody is really driving; be exhaustive rather than tidy.
3. How is TA structured today — centralized, regional, embedded, hybrid — and how many recruiters and coordinators sit in it? Describe any specialization (early talent, frontline, professional, executive) and whether recruiters report into TA or into local HR.
4. What tooling is TA running on right now? Name the ATS, CRM, sourcing, assessment, scheduling, interview-support/AI, and employer-brand tools, and flag any I think are underused.
5. In my leadership's own words — not the metrics I wish they used — what hiring outcomes do they actually judge TA on? Paraphrase how my CHRO, CPO, or business leaders describe success, and name any number they repeat.
6. What has TA already tried and quietly abandoned in the last two or three years? Name at least one initiative, what happened to it, and whether it was ever formally ended or just faded.
7. Describe the bandwidth and budget reality in a sentence or two: how much team capacity goes to day-to-day requisition work versus initiative work, what budget exists for anything new, and whether headcount is growing, flat, or shrinking.
8. Who actually has to agree before an initiative gets killed — me alone, my CHRO, a regional CPO, a business sponsor, procurement for a tool contract? Name the roles and how much friction each adds.
9. What recurring forum already exists where this could be reviewed — a monthly TA leadership meeting, a quarterly business review, an HR ops meeting — who attends, how long is it, and what gets discussed there today? If nothing suitable exists, say so.
10. Which initiative on my list will be politically hard to retire because it's someone's pet project or has a visible sponsor — name it and whose it is — and separately, what's my own biggest frustration with how TA initiatives run today, in my own words?

**If one of my answers is thin or generic** (a one-word answer, something that could apply to any TA team, no named initiative, owner, number, or example), don't silently accept it and don't block me either. Nudge me once: say what's missing, give one concrete example of the detail that would help, and explicitly offer me the choice to add more or move on as-is. Never nudge more than once on the same question — if my second answer is still thin, or I say move on, drop it immediately and continue.

**Never give me a generic template with placeholders.** Everything you produce must visibly use my actual initiative names, my actual owners, my actual metrics, my actual review forum and approval chain.

**Then recap and confirm.** Once intake is done, summarize back in a short paragraph what you understood — my initiative list, my structure, the outcomes leadership judges TA on, my bandwidth and political constraints — and ask me to confirm or correct it before you draft anything.

## Then build it with me, in this order

1. **Build the exhaustive initiative inventory.** Using my initiative list plus anything visible in the documents I uploaded, write out every active item as a row, including the ones I hesitated over. Add anything my documents imply but I didn't name — a tool with a live contract, a campus program mid-season, a pilot referenced in an old deck — and ask me to confirm or delete each. Then count the rows out loud: most TA leaders name six or seven initiatives from memory and end up with fifteen or twenty on paper.

2. **Classify each initiative and name one accountable owner.** Tag every row by pillar — strategy, enablement (tooling, process, automation), or execution/delivery — and separately as a program, tool, pilot, or process change. Then assign exactly one named person as accountable: not a team, not a co-lead pair. Where I gave you no clear owner, or one person owns five rows, flag it rather than paper over it — unowned and over-owned initiatives are the two states that most reliably produce drift.

3. **Attach two to three real outcome metrics per initiative.** Draw these from the outcomes my leadership actually judges TA on, in their own words from intake — candidate experience, speed through named process stages, offer acceptance, quality or retention of hires, recruiter capability, cost per hire. Every metric must be something my ATS, survey tool, or funnel export can produce today, or something I commit to instrumenting. If an initiative has no metric that survives that test, mark it "unmeasurable as scoped" rather than inventing a proxy — that label is itself a decision.

4. **Establish a baseline and a decision horizon for each row.** Record today's number for every metric from the data I gave you, or note honestly that no baseline exists. Then set the date by which I'll make a scale/hold/kill call. Horizons should differ by type: an enablement pilot may need a quarter, a campus or employer-brand program a full cycle. A row with no baseline gets a shorter horizon whose first job is establishing one.

5. **Write the scale / hold / kill thresholds in advance.** For each initiative, state in plain numbers what result triggers scaling (more regions, segments, or recruiters, with more budget or headcount), what means hold and iterate one more horizon, and what means kill. Write these *before* scoring anything, tell me to get them agreed by the approval chain I named, and record who agreed. This is the load-bearing step: thresholds set in advance turn a political conversation into a factual one — the room is no longer arguing whether an initiative deserves to live, only checking a number against a line it already signed off on.

6. **Run the first scoring pass and produce the ranked portfolio view.** Score every row against its own thresholds and output the finished scorecard as a table: initiative, type, owner, metrics with baseline and current, horizon, threshold, resulting call. Rank it — scale candidates top, holds middle, kill candidates bottom — and list the "unmeasurable as scoped" and unowned rows separately, since those need a scoping decision rather than a performance verdict. Score the politically sensitive initiative I named honestly, with a note on how to handle its sponsor instead of a softened number.

7. **Stand up the review cadence and the retirement ritual.** Using the forum I named — or proposing a new one if none fits — set where the portfolio gets reviewed, how often, who attends, and how much agenda time it takes; monthly review with a deeper quarterly reset works for most functions. Then write the retirement ritual: how a killed initiative gets announced in whatever recurring TA communication I already have, what happens to its tool contract and in-flight commitments, what gets said to its sponsor, and — the part everyone skips — where the freed capacity goes, named recruiter by named recruiter. Bandwidth that isn't formally reclaimed evaporates, and the next initiative launches against the same overcommitted team.

8. **Offer to persist this context for next time.** At the end, tell me you now have a working profile of my TA function — structure, initiative list, tooling, outcome metrics, approval chain, review forum — and offer me two options: (1) save this context in a project or custom instructions so future requests build on it, or (2) rewrite this whole prompt with my answers written into a "Known Context" section at the top so it's baked in and I never re-answer intake.

## What "done" looks like

A single populated scorecard lists every active TA initiative by its real name, one accountable person per row and no unowned row left unflagged; each carries two to three outcome metrics tied to what leadership actually judges TA on, each with a real baseline or an explicit note that none exists; every row has a dated decision horizon and written scale/hold/kill thresholds agreed by the named approval chain *before* the first scoring pass; that pass has been run and the portfolio ranked into scale candidates, holds, kill candidates, and a separate list of unmeasurable or unowned rows needing a scoping decision; the politically sensitive initiative is scored honestly with a stated approach to its sponsor rather than quietly exempted; a named forum owns the review on a named cadence with the portfolio on its written agenda; and a retirement ritual covers announcement, contract cleanup, the sponsor conversation, and reassignment of freed capacity to named people.

---

## Pitfalls — avoid all of these

- Building the inventory from memory instead of reconciling it against the ATS, tool contracts, and old roadmap decks — the initiatives nobody remembers are the ones quietly consuming recruiter time.
- Attaching activity metrics (sessions delivered, tool logins, posts published) instead of hiring outcome metrics, which lets an initiative look healthy while moving nothing leadership cares about.
- Assigning an initiative to a team, a function, or two co-leads rather than one named person, which produces a row that gets reviewed for three quarters and never decided.
- Setting the thresholds after seeing the first scores, which turns the review into a negotiation and guarantees well-sponsored initiatives survive regardless of performance.
- Quietly exempting the pet project instead of scoring it honestly and planning the sponsor conversation — one visible exemption teaches everyone that the thresholds are optional.
- Killing an initiative without reclaiming its bandwidth, so the freed capacity never lands anywhere and the team stays as thin as it was before the prune.
- Giving every initiative the same decision horizon, which either kills seasonal work like campus or frontline hiring before a full cycle has run, or lets an enablement pilot drift a year past the point where the answer was clear.
- Forgetting to offer to persist context at the end.
