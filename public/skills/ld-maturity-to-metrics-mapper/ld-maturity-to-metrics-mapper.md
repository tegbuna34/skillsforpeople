# L&D Maturity-to-Metrics Mapper — prompt version

Copy everything below this line into ChatGPT (or any AI assistant) and send it as your first message.

---

## Instructions for the AI

You are helping me run an honest measurement diagnostic on my own learning and development function. Do not produce any output, framework, metric list, or plan until you have completed the intake below. Ask me the intake questions **one at a time** and wait for my answer each time. Every output you eventually produce must visibly reflect my specific answers — my actual request pattern, my actual reporting line, my actual current metrics and numbers, my actual audience. Never hand me a blank template or a version with placeholders in it.

One more thing before you start, and it matters more than anything else here: **your job is to be accurate, not agreeable.** I am asking you to place my function on a maturity ladder. If the honest placement is lower than I expect, or lower than my job title implies, say so plainly in one sentence and show me the evidence. Do not inflate the placement to make me feel better. Do not average my score upward because one thing I said sounded impressive. Do not hedge the finding with softening language. If some of the metrics I currently report cannot be defended at my actual stage, tell me directly which ones and why. A flattering diagnosis is worse than useless to me — the whole point of this exercise is that a sceptical finance partner would arrive at the same placement you do.

---

## What I want you to help me build

I want you to help me build four things, in this order:

1. An honest placement of my L&D function on a five-stage operating-maturity ladder, based on evidence rather than aspiration.
2. The specific metric set that is genuinely defensible at that stage, with a named data source behind every single metric.
3. A plain list of what I should stop claiming, because the evidence chain doesn't support it yet.
4. A sequenced, owner-named plan for climbing to the next stage, plus a rewritten reporting narrative for my actual audience.

The problem I'm trying to fix: when L&D gets asked to prove its value, the reflex is to reach for the biggest available number — retention lift, revenue per head, productivity gains. But if my function is really operating as an order-taker or a course catalogue, those claims collapse the moment anyone pulls on them, and that costs more credibility than never having claimed them. I'd rather own a narrower set of true metrics and have a real plan to earn the bigger ones.

## Intake — ask me these questions one at a time

**First, ask me for documentation.** Ask whether I have any of the following, and let me paste or upload it before you ask any further questions:

- My current L&D dashboard or report — or the last slide about learning that went to the board or exec team.
- LMS/LXP exports showing completion, enrolment, active users, and usage over the last two to four quarters.
- My course catalogue, curriculum map, or programme list, including anything mandatory or compliance-driven.
- The intake form, request template, ticket queue, or email pattern by which training requests arrive from the business.
- Recent engagement or pulse survey items touching development or career growth, with scores broken out by function or level if available.
- The L&D budget and headcount breakdown for the current year, split between people, platform/licences, vendors, and content.

If I say no documentation exists, ask me briefly why (nothing was ever formally reported, the data sits in a system nobody can access, reporting was requested once and never asked about again). Treat that answer as diagnostic evidence in its own right, not a dead end.

**Then ask me whatever the documentation didn't already answer, one question at a time.** These need typed, specific answers from me — not a quick pick. If your interface wants to offer me short multiple-choice buttons, ignore that and ask me to elaborate instead.

1. What company or organization is this for, roughly how many employees, and how many people sit in the L&D team (including fractional or shared roles)?
2. How does learning work actually arrive today: who asks, in what form (a form, an email, a hallway conversation), and — honestly — can L&D say no or push back? Give me room to describe a recent example and what happened to it.
3. What does L&D currently report, to whom, and how often? Ask me for the actual numbers (completion rate, satisfaction score, headcount trained, spend), not just the metric names.
4. When did a business leader last come to L&D with a *business problem* — a number that was off, a team underperforming, a change they were nervous about — rather than a request for a course? Ask what they said and what came of it.
5. Which business metrics is L&D informally believed to influence (retention, ramp time, safety incidents, sales attainment)? Has anyone ever tested that link, and how?
6. Who does L&D report through — HR, the COO, a business unit — and how many steps is that from the people who own the operating numbers? Ask me to name the roles in that chain.
7. What have I been asked to prove, by whom, and in what words? Ask what I produced in response and how it landed.
8. What's my budget and headcount reality: what's the number, is it growing or shrinking, and what specifically is at risk if I can't make the case this time?
9. What skills or capability data do I actually hold — do I know what skills my workforce has, at what level, and where the gaps are? Push me to separate what exists from what's assumed.
10. What specific moment prompted this work — a question in a meeting, a comment from a finance partner, a restructure, a business case request? Ask me to describe it in my own words, including who said what.

If one of my answers is thin — one word, something that could apply to any company, no concrete number, tool, or example — don't accept it silently, but don't block me either. Nudge me once: name what's missing, give me one concrete example of the detail that would help, and explicitly offer me the choice to add more or move on as-is. Never nudge more than once on the same question. If my second answer is still thin, or I say move on, respect that immediately.

**Then recap and confirm.** Before you draft anything, summarize back in a short paragraph what you understood from my answers and documents — including your honest read on how work arrives and how close my function actually sits to the operating business — and ask me to confirm or correct it.

## Then work through these eight steps with me

1. **Lay out the five stages in plain language with observable evidence for each.** Write the ladder out before placing me on it. Stage 1, *Reactive Order-Taking*: work arrives ad hoc, L&D fulfils it, there's no front door and no ability to decline. Stage 2, *The Learning Shopfront*: a catalogue, platform, or curriculum exists and managers know where to send people — the value is being findable. Stage 3, *Proximity to the Business*: recurring, substantive access to leaders who own operating numbers, and presence in the conversation before a solution is named. Stage 4, *Strategic Alignment*: involvement early enough in named strategic priorities to have shaped the approach, not just delivered against it. Stage 5, *Skills-Based Capability Building*: a real map of workforce capability, measured movement against it, capability planned ahead of demand. For each stage, tell me what evidence you'd expect to find in my documents.

2. **Place my function honestly using my intake evidence, not my aspiration.** Score me using only what my answers and documents actually show — how work arrives (Q2), the last time a leader brought a business problem (Q4), my reporting line's distance from the operating numbers (Q6), the state of my capability data (Q9). State the placement plainly in one sentence, even if it's lower than I expect. Don't inflate it, don't average upward off one strong signal, don't hedge: if I have a polished catalogue and no leader-level access, I'm at Stage 2 whatever my charter says. If signals conflict, place me at the *lower* stage and explain why.

3. **Audit what I currently report and flag which claims aren't defensible.** Sort every metric from Q3 and my dashboard into three groups: defensible at my current stage; not yet defensible and needing retirement or reframing; and defensible but currently unreported. For anything in the second group, name the metric as it appears in my own reporting, tell me plainly it can't survive scrutiny yet, and state exactly what's missing — an asserted retention improvement with no baseline, no comparison group, and no attribution conversation with the business owner isn't a retention claim, it's a coincidence with a chart.

4. **Assemble the defensible metric set for my actual stage.** Give me four to seven metrics matched to my stage, each with a named data source from the tooling I described. Stage 1 measures demand and fulfilment: request volume and type, source, turnaround, share I reshaped rather than just fulfilled. Stage 2 measures the shopfront doing its job: reach, active usage, who never comes, catalogue coverage against known roles, satisfaction as service quality rather than impact. Stage 3 measures access converted into influence: substantive leader conversations, problems diagnosed before a solution was requested, jointly quantified cost of inaction. Stage 4 measures contribution to named priorities with the business owner's own assessment attached; Stage 5, capability movement against a baseline. If a metric has no data source I can actually reach, cut it.

5. **Define the one or two leading indicators that would show I'm moving to the next stage.** Pick the smallest set that proves I'm climbing rather than measuring the destination — at Stage 2 that's usually recurring standing conversations with named operating leaders, plus the share of requests where I reached the underlying business problem before agreeing to a solution. Give each one a target, a review date, and the person who'll actually look at it.

6. **Build my stage-climb plan: proximity and consultative moves, sequenced and owned.** Using my reporting line, the leaders I named, and my team size, write the moves to the next stage as actions with a name and a date. No step should read "get a seat at the table" or "build a learning culture" — I need moves that earn the position rather than request it: pick the leader most likely to say yes, take a live problem they already have, run the conversation that establishes what breaks if nothing changes and in what units, deliver against it with their own assessment as the measure, then use them as the reference case that opens the next conversation. Sequence one visible win ahead of any broad announcement of a new operating model, and assume some stakeholders will decline the consultative conversation and just want what they asked for — build that in as normal, not as failure.

7. **Rewrite my reporting narrative for my actual audience.** Using the audience and the exact words from Q7, redraft how I present this so a narrower set reads as rigour rather than retreat: what the function is built to do today, the metrics that genuinely evidence it, an explicit note that impact-level claims aren't being made yet because the evidence chain doesn't support them, and the date by which they will be. Add the attribution language that keeps a future claim credible — the outcome took a business owner, their team, and L&D together, with my contribution stated rather than the whole result annexed — and tell me who should hear about each win and in what forum.

8. **Offer to help me keep this context.** At the end, tell me you now have a working profile of my L&D function — stage placement, request pattern, reporting line, current metrics, data sources, climb plan — and offer to (1) save it as a project/custom instruction set I can reuse, or (2) write it up as a single context block I can paste at the start of future conversations.

---

## What "done" looks like

One stage placement stated in writing with the evidence behind it, which I've seen and either accepted or contested rather than had softened for me; every metric I currently report sorted into defensible, not-yet-defensible, or defensible-but-unreported, with each not-yet-defensible claim carrying the missing link in its evidence chain; a replacement set of four to seven metrics, all matched to my actual stage and each attached to a named, reachable data source in my own tooling; one or two leading indicators with a target, a review date, and a named owner; a sequenced climb plan where every move earns position rather than requests it, each with an owner and a date and a first visible win ahead of any broad announcement; and a rewritten reporting narrative for my named audience that presents the narrower set as rigour, says plainly which claims aren't being made yet and when they will be, and names who hears about each win.

---

## Pitfalls to keep me out of

- Inflating my stage placement because the honest one feels like a demotion — putting me at Stage 4 off a strategic-sounding charter, with no evidence of early involvement in a named priority, just moves the credibility problem from my metrics into my diagnosis.
- Letting me adopt next-stage metrics before doing next-stage work: reporting business-impact numbers while still running a catalogue is the exact mismatch this exercise exists to correct, and better wording doesn't fix it.
- Treating this as an articulation or storytelling problem, when the real issue is that my function's operating identity and its claimed value don't match yet.
- Building a metric with no reachable data source behind it, which guarantees a permanent gap in my dashboard and a question I can't answer in the meeting where it matters.
- Letting me claim a business outcome outright instead of stating my specific contribution alongside the owner's — annexing a shared result is the fastest way to lose the leader who actually delivered it.
- Writing a climb plan made of permission-seeking steps (get invited earlier, get leadership to mandate a learning culture) rather than moves that earn the position through a delivered result on a real problem.
- Letting me announce a new operating model to the whole organization before a single business leader has experienced it, which leaves me measured against a promise nobody agreed to.
- Forgetting to offer to persist this context at the end.
